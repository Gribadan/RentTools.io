On September 29, 2026, Vrbo emailed its hosts a single sentence that matters more than anything else it will send this year: from **October 29**, every host moves to "a single, transparent 12% commission fee." For a pay-per-booking host without a property-management system, that replaces roughly 8%. For a host connected through a PMS, it replaces 5%. Same listings, same guests, and Vrbo's cut grows by half — or more than doubles.

If Vrbo is 20% of your bookings, this is a few hundred to a few thousand dollars a year per listing, and you have until the 29th to decide what to do about it. Here is the math, the clause in the new terms that limits how you can respond, and the order I would do things in.

## TL;DR

- From **October 29, 2026**, Vrbo charges a flat **12%** host commission, replacing ~8% (no PMS) or 5% (PMS).
- Vrbo's FAQ says the 12% **includes payment processing**; some regions, Europe included, run **12–15%**.
- To net the same per booking, raise Vrbo rates **4.5%** (from 8%) or **8%** (from 5%).
- The new Host Terms include **rate-parity language** — a Vrbo-only price hike may breach it.
- On $24,000 a year of Vrbo revenue, the change costs **$960–$1,680** before you reprice.
- Vrbo at 12% is still cheaper than Airbnb host-only at 15% — don't delist in a panic.

## What actually changes on October 29

Until now, Vrbo ran two compensation models side by side.

**Pay-per-booking (PPB)** was the default: a **5% commission** on the booking plus a **3% payment-processing fee**, so roughly **8%** all in. Hosts who connected through a software partner — a PMS or channel manager with a Vrbo API connection — were widely reported to pay **5%**, because the processing line was waived or folded into the partner deal.

**Annual subscription** was the legacy model: a flat yearly fee per listing, widely reported at **$499–$699**, with no per-booking commission. It has been closed to new hosts for years.

From October 29, both collapse into one number. The headline is **12% per booking**. Vrbo's own FAQ, as republished on Expedia Group's newsroom, says the 12% **includes payment processing** for pay-per-booking hosts, and that the rate "may vary regionally and across Vrbo's global heritage brands, ranging from 12–15%." Industry coverage names **Europe, Australia, and New Zealand** as places where the rate can sit above 12%. The authoritative number for your listing is the one on your **Owner Dashboard**, not the email.

Two loose ends worth knowing before you build a spreadsheet on top of this.

**Processing may not be fully settled.** The new Host Terms of Service still describe "a commission" and "a payment processing fee" as separate charges, and the Accommodation Fee Collection Agreement says any processing rate will be published on a separate disclosure page. The FAQ says 12% is all in. I would plan on 12% all in and verify it on the first payout statement after the 29th.

**Subscriptions are on borrowed time.** The new terms say Vrbo "is not accepting new Subscription Listings and may not allow the renewal of prior Subscription Listings." If you are a subscriber, assume your next renewal date is the day you move to 12%.

The guest side does not change. Travelers still see a separate Vrbo service fee at checkout, calculated as a percentage of the reservation before taxes and refundable deposits. That fee was never yours and still isn't — but it does mean the guest's total already carries a platform markup before you touch your rate.

## The gross-up math: how much to raise Vrbo rates

The intuitive move is to add the four extra points to your price: 8% → 12%, so raise rates 4%. That under-corrects, because the new fee is charged on the new, higher price. The correct factor is:

**new rate = old rate × (1 − old fee) ÷ (1 − new fee)**

| Your situation | Old fee | New fee | Raise rates by | $200 night becomes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PPB, no PMS | 8% | 12% | 4.5% | $209 |
| PPB, PMS-connected | 5% | 12% | 8.0% | $216 |
| PPB, no PMS, 15% region | 8% | 15% | 8.2% | $216.50 |
| PPB, PMS, 15% region | 5% | 15% | 11.8% | $223.50 |

Check the first row: $209 × 0.88 = $183.92, and $200 × 0.92 = $184.00. Same net, within eight cents.

Apply the factor to every fee you put on a Vrbo reservation, not just the nightly rate. Assume the 12% lands on the cleaning fee, pet fee, and any other host-set charge until your statement says otherwise — the old 5% commission did. A $120 cleaning fee that netted $110.40 at 8% nets $105.60 at 12%. That's $4.80 per stay, quietly, on a cost you were trying to recover at par. The [cleaning-fee vs all-in pricing](/blog/airbnb-cleaning-fee-vs-all-in-pricing) trade-off just shifted a little on Vrbo.

## What it costs you per year

Take a realistic Vrbo slice: a family-sized listing doing **20 Vrbo bookings a year at a $1,200 average booking value** — **$24,000** of Vrbo revenue. Vrbo's audience skews toward longer, multi-night family stays, so the booking values run higher than Airbnb's for the same property.

| Situation | Fee before | Fee after | Before | After | Annual cost of the change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PPB, no PMS | 8% | 12% | $1,920 | $2,880 | **$960** |
| PPB, PMS-connected | 5% | 12% | $1,200 | $2,880 | **$1,680** |
| PPB, no PMS, 15% region | 8% | 15% | $1,920 | $3,600 | **$1,680** |
| Subscriber at $699/yr + 3% processing | ~5.9% | 12% | $1,419 | $2,880 | **$1,461** |

On a single $1,000 booking, the PMS-connected host pays $120 instead of $50. That's the number to keep in your head: **every thousand dollars of Vrbo revenue now costs $40–$70 more** in most of the US, and up to $100 more where the 15% rate applies.

The subscriber line deserves its own note. At 12%, a $699 subscription breaks even at **$5,825 of annual Vrbo revenue**. Most subscribers who kept the plan this long did so because they were well above that. They are the hosts who lose the most in absolute dollars, and the ones with the least warning, because their renewal date — not October 29 — is when the bill arrives.

## The catch: the parity clause in the new terms

Here is why "just raise the Vrbo price 4.5%" is not the whole answer.

The new Host Terms that take effect on the same day include parity language. Section 4.5.7 asks hosts to keep content "at least as complete, accurate, current, detailed, and favorable" as on other channels. Section 5.5 defines content to include **rates, discounts, fees, taxes, and cancellation policies**, and separately covers add-ons like early check-in, parking, and pet fees. The channels named include **your own website and booking engine**, your PMS, and any third-party channel. Section 12.7.2 says your listing may not be materially more favorable elsewhere unless the law requires it or Vrbo agrees in writing. Section 7.1 makes "rate consistency" a factor in listing quality scores.

Read plainly, that means a Vrbo price that sits 4.5–8% above your Airbnb or direct price is exactly what the terms tell you not to do. Two caveats, stated honestly: Vrbo hasn't said how it will compare prices across channels or what enforcement looks like, and there are unconfirmed reports that Vrbo may relax the direct-website match. In the EU, the Digital Markets Act already stops Booking.com from enforcing parity — but Vrbo is not a designated gatekeeper, so that protection doesn't extend to it.

The practical reading: if you raise only Vrbo, the realistic risk is not a lawsuit, it's a quieter ranking penalty through the "rate consistency" quality factor. That trade can still be worth making. Just make it knowingly.

## Your four options

**1. Raise Vrbo only, by the gross-up factor.** Cleanest math, full recovery, parity exposure. Reasonable if Vrbo is under 15% of your bookings and you can afford some ranking softness.

**2. Raise every channel by a blended amount.** If Vrbo is 20% of revenue and you need +4.5% there, a **0.9% increase across all channels** recovers the same dollars and keeps prices identical everywhere. Small enough that it rarely moves conversion; the [weekend premium](/blog/weekend-pricing-premium-math) and seasonal ladder you already run swamp it.

**3. Absorb it.** If your Vrbo bookings are filling nights that would otherwise sit empty — shoulder season, long family stays, mid-week gaps — the right test is contribution margin, not the commission rate. A night that earns $180 after cleaning and utilities still earns $172 at the higher fee; a night that stays empty earns $0. The [off-season contribution-margin math](/blog/off-season-close-or-stay-open-math) is the same logic.

**4. Pull back from Vrbo.** Only if Vrbo bookings are mostly *displacing* Airbnb or direct bookings you would have gotten anyway. Most hosts can't show that, and should not assume it.

I'd do option 2 for most hosts with Vrbo at 10–30% of revenue, and option 1 only for listings where Vrbo is a minor channel.

The last time a platform moved my fee, I told myself I'd reprice "after the season" and didn't touch the rate for 11 weeks. On one listing that handed the platform about $400 I didn't need to give it. The math above takes 20 minutes. Do it before the 29th, not after the first statement.

Vrbo just went from the cheapest major OTA to the middle of the pack. It did not become expensive. The hosts who lose the most are the PMS-connected ones, because their 5% deal was the best rate on the market and it's gone.

## The checklist before October 29

1. **Open the Owner Dashboard** and find the rate shown for each listing. If it says 15%, use the 15% rows above.
2. **Pull 12 months of Vrbo payouts** and total the booking value. Multiply by the fee delta. That's your number.
3. **Check your Vrbo share** of total revenue. Under 15%: option 1 is fine. 15–40%: option 2. Over 40%: run the contribution-margin test on Vrbo nights before you change anything.
4. **Update rates in one place.** If you push rates through a PMS or channel manager, change the base there so channels stay in step. If you edit Vrbo by hand, update cleaning and pet fees too.
5. **Mark the first post-October-29 payout** in your calendar and reconcile it line by line: commission rate, whether processing appears as a separate line, and whether bookings made before the 29th were charged the old rate.
6. **Keep calendar sync independent of the fee model.** Losing the PMS discount doesn't change the need to keep Vrbo, Airbnb, and Booking.com calendars in step. iCal sync still works on Vrbo, and [RentTools does it for free](/onboard) — so the only thing you're paying Vrbo for is the booking. If your feeds have been flaky, the [calendar-not-syncing checklist](/blog/airbnb-calendar-not-syncing) applies to Vrbo too.

## FAQ

**When does the Vrbo 12% commission start?**
October 29, 2026. Vrbo notified hosts by email on September 29, 2026, and the new Host Terms of Service and Accommodation Fee Collection Agreement take effect on the same day. Continuing to use Vrbo after October 29 counts as accepting the new terms.

**Does the Vrbo 12% include the payment processing fee?**
According to Vrbo's FAQ, yes: pay-per-booking hosts pay a flat 12% that includes processing. The new Host Terms still list a commission and a processing fee as separate items, so check your first payout statement after October 29 to confirm there is no separate processing line on your listing.

**Is the new Vrbo commission 12% everywhere?**
No. Vrbo says the rate ranges from 12% to 15% depending on region and brand, and reports name Europe, Australia, and New Zealand as markets where it can be higher. Your Owner Dashboard shows the rate that applies to your listing.

**How much should I raise my Vrbo prices to cover the 12% fee?**
To keep the same net payout, multiply your rate by (1 − old fee) ÷ (1 − new fee). Going from 8% to 12% means raising rates 4.5%; going from 5% to 12% means raising them 8%. Simply adding four percentage points under-corrects because the new fee is charged on the higher price.

**Can I charge more on Vrbo than on Airbnb or my own website?**
The new Host Terms include parity language saying your Vrbo rates, fees, and policies should be at least as favorable as on other channels, including your own website, and they make rate consistency a factor in listing quality. Vrbo hasn't said how it will enforce this, but a Vrbo-only price increase carries a real risk of lower ranking.

**What happens to my Vrbo annual subscription?**
Vrbo is not accepting new subscription listings and says it may not renew existing ones. Plan for your listing to move to the 12% per-booking model at your next renewal date. At 12%, a $699 subscription only beats per-booking pricing below about $5,825 of annual Vrbo revenue.

**Is Vrbo still worth it at 12%?**
For most hosts, yes. At 12% Vrbo still takes less from the host than Airbnb's 15% host-only fee or Booking.com's real take rate of around 19% for non-VAT-registered EU hosts. The real question is whether your Vrbo bookings fill nights that would otherwise stay empty; if they do, the higher fee is still a good trade.

**Do bookings made before October 29 pay the old Vrbo commission?**
Vrbo's public material doesn't spell this out for stays booked before the 29th that check in after it. Treat it as unknown and reconcile your first payout statement after October 29 line by line to see which rate was applied to existing reservations.

## One opinionated take

Vrbo didn't become expensive on October 29. It stopped being cheap, and it removed the one discount — the PMS rate — that rewarded hosts for running a tight operation. The hosts who will feel it most are the ones who never looked at their Vrbo payouts because the fee was too small to matter. It's big enough to matter now. Spend 20 minutes with last year's payouts before the 29th, pick a repricing option on purpose, and let the hosts who wait for the first statement subsidise the ones who didn't.
