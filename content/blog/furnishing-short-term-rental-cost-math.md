I furnished my first rental for $4,200 and thought I'd been clever. Then the third guest wrote that the mattress "felt like a folded towel on plywood," and I spent another $700 replacing the exact thing I'd saved $400 on. The furnishing budget wasn't too small. It was aimed at the wrong line items — big on a coffee table nobody mentions, thin on the one surface a guest spends eight hours a night touching.

This post is the number I wish I'd had before I bought anything: what furnishing a short-term rental from empty actually costs, itemized; how the cost per bedroom moves as the place gets bigger; how many booked nights it takes to earn the money back; and the part almost every host forgets — that furniture wears out, so the one-time bill is really a per-night cost hiding inside your break-even.

## TL;DR

- Budget ~$7,000–$7,500 to furnish a 1-bed rental from empty.
- The first bedroom is the expensive one; each extra bed costs less.
- Spend where reviews are made: mattress, wifi, blackout, water pressure.
- Go cheap on decor — it photographs the same at a tenth the price.
- Furniture isn't $0/night: amortized over ~5 years it's ~$7–8/night.
- Buy 3 linen sets per bed so laundry never blocks a same-day turnover.

## What "furnished" actually means to a guest

Before a single dollar, sort every purchase into one of three tiers, because the tier — not the price tag — tells you where to spend.

**Tier 1: review-critical.** The handful of things a bad guest review will name directly. The mattress. The wifi. Blackout in the bedroom. Water pressure and a shower that runs hot. A kitchen with enough real cookware to make dinner. Pillows. Heating and cooling that works. These are non-negotiable and you buy them once, properly, because a 4-star review for "bed was uncomfortable" costs you more over a year than the price gap between a good mattress and a bad one — and it's [nearly impossible to outrun at a low review count](/blog/airbnb-rating-recovery-math).

**Tier 2: durability-critical.** High-touch items that guests use harder than an owner ever would: the sofa, dining chairs, drawer slides, the shower curtain rod, the kettle. A guest doesn't baby your furniture. Buy these one notch above what you'd buy for yourself, ideally in a commercial or contract grade, because the cheap version doesn't survive 200 turnovers a year and you'll rebuy it inside eighteen months.

**Tier 3: aesthetic.** Art, cushions, the rug, decorative bits, the console table nobody sits at. This is where hosts blow their budget and it's exactly where the money doesn't matter — a $40 framed print and a $400 original photograph render identically in a listing photo and in a guest's memory. Spend Tier-1 money here and you've furnished your ego, not your business.

Get the tiers right and a modest budget outperforms a lavish one. Get them backwards — the way I did — and you pay twice.

## The itemized budget: furnishing a one-bedroom from empty

Here's a realistic build for a one-bedroom that sleeps two to four (sofa bed in the living room), bought new at mid-range prices in a mid-cost market. Prices are round and in USD; scale them to your city and to the tier logic above.

| Category | Budget | Tier / note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bed: mattress + frame + protector | $850 | Tier 1 — the line I got wrong first |
| Bedroom storage: dresser + 2 nightstands | $550 | Tier 3 — buy used, nobody scores drawers |
| Sofa (ideally a sofa bed) | $900 | Tier 2 — contract grade, adds sleep capacity |
| Coffee table + TV stand + living rug | $500 | mixed — rug is Tier 2, the rest Tier 3 |
| Dining table + 4 chairs | $450 | Tier 2 on the chairs |
| TV + wifi router + streaming stick | $500 | Tier 1 on the router |
| Kitchen: cookware, dishes, cutlery, glasses | $400 | Tier 1 — enough to cook a real meal |
| Small appliances: kettle, toaster, coffee, microwave | $350 | Tier 1 — coffee is not optional |
| Linens: 3 bedding sets + protectors | $300 | Tier 1 — three per bed, see below |
| Towels: 3 bath sets + kitchen towels | $180 | Tier 1 — white, always white |
| Blackout curtains + rods, all rooms | $350 | Tier 1 — the cheapest review-saver there is |
| Lighting: lamps + warm bulbs | $200 | Tier 3 |
| Decor: art, mirror, plants, cushions | $500 | Tier 3 — cap this, it's where budgets die |
| Bathroom kit: shower curtain, bins, hairdryer, scale | $150 | Tier 2 on the curtain rod |
| Cleaning kit: vacuum, mop, iron + board, drying rack | $350 | Tier 2 — a turnover tool, not decor |
| Safety: smoke + CO alarms, extinguisher, first aid, smart lock | $370 | Tier 1 — legally and morally non-negotiable |
| Welcome + misc: guidebook, batteries, tools, hangers | $150 | the long tail that always shows up |

That totals about **$7,050**. Add a 7% buffer for delivery, flat-pack assembly, and the two or three things you'll buy twice, and you land at roughly **$7,500** for a one-bed done right. The smart lock line alone is worth its own [cost breakdown vs a lockbox](/blog/smart-lock-vs-lockbox-cost-math); the rest is the stuff no single post ever adds up.

Note what's *not* in here: consumables. Toilet paper, coffee, soap, and dish tabs are a per-stay operating cost, not furnishing — and they have [their own budget line most hosts forget](/blog/consumables-cost-per-stay-math). Keep the two apart or your capex number swallows a recurring cost and both go fuzzy.

## Cost per bedroom: why the second bedroom is cheaper than the first

The intuitive assumption is that a 2-bed costs twice a 1-bed. It doesn't, and the reason is that most of the money is in the shared rooms. The kitchen, the living room, the single bathroom kit, the wifi, the cleaning gear, the safety equipment — you buy all of it once whether the place has one bedroom or three. A second bedroom adds a bed, three more linen sets, a dresser, two nightstands, and a few more towels. That's it.

| Property | Total furnishing | Per bedroom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Studio | ~$6,000 | $6,000 |
| 1-bed | ~$7,050 | $7,050 |
| 2-bed | ~$10,500 | $5,250 |
| 3-bed | ~$13,500 | $4,500 |

The studio barely undercuts the one-bed because you still need every room's worth of stuff minus a wall. But from there the curve bends: the marginal bedroom runs **$3,000 to $3,500**, not $7,000, so the per-bedroom average keeps falling as the place gets bigger. This is the same shape that makes a bigger listing more capital-efficient per booked head — more sleeping capacity spread over shared rooms you only furnish once. If you're deciding how many beds to cram in, that marginal-bedroom number is the honest input, and it pairs directly with [the break-even nightly rate math](/blog/break-even-nightly-rate-math) for the extra guests those beds bring.

## The payback: how many nights until the furniture is free

Take the one-bed at $7,500 furnished. Say it books at an average $95 a night and runs 55% occupancy — about 200 booked nights a year, roughly $19,000 gross.

The crude payback is easy: $7,500 ÷ $95 ≈ **79 booked nights of gross revenue**. At 55% occupancy that's about fourteen weeks of calendar — under four months from your first booking — before the furniture has paid for itself in top-line terms.

That number is a useful gut check and a lie by omission. Gross isn't profit; cleaning, platform commission, utilities, and consumables eat a chunk of every night before anything reaches the furniture. Measured against actual contribution, the honest payback on a one-bed's furnishing is closer to **six to nine months** of operation in a healthy market. Still fast. Furnishing is one of the highest-return checks you'll write as a host — the capital comes back inside a year and then the same furniture earns for years more. The mistake isn't spending the $7,500. The mistake is treating it as spent once and forgetting it keeps a running tab.

## Furnishing is not a one-time cost — the replacement cycle

Here's the line that separates hosts who quietly lose money from hosts who don't. Furniture wears out on a schedule, and a guest-occupied property wears it out faster than a home. Linens go grey and pill. The sofa sags. The kettle furs up and dies. If you booked the $7,500 as a one-time cost in year one and never thought about it again, your break-even is quietly wrong every night after.

Amortize the build over each item's real replacement life and the one-time number becomes a per-night one:

| Item group | Capex | Replace every | Per year |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mattress + bed frame | $850 | 6 yr | $142 |
| Sofa + soft seating | $900 | 8 yr | $113 |
| Case goods (tables, chairs, dressers, stands) | $1,500 | 10 yr | $150 |
| Electronics + appliances (TV, router, kettle) | $850 | 4 yr | $213 |
| Kitchen + dishware | $400 | 5 yr | $80 |
| Linens + towels | $480 | 1.5 yr | $320 |
| Decor, rugs, curtains, lamps | $1,050 | 4 yr | $263 |
| Cleaning + safety gear | $870 | 5 yr | $174 |
| **Total** | **$6,900** | — | **~$1,455/yr** |

Spread that $1,455 a year across 200 booked nights and furnishing costs you **about $7.30 a night** — every night, forever, whether you feel it or not. That's the number that belongs in your break-even, sitting right next to the [maintenance reserve most hosts also set too low](/blog/maintenance-reserve-short-term-rental-math).

Read the linens row twice. It's the smallest capex line — $480 — and by a distance the biggest annual cost, because bedding and towels turn over every eighteen months under guest use and hot-wash cycles while everything else lasts years. That's also why you buy **three sets per bed**, not one or two: a same-day turnover can't wait on a wash cycle, and a stripped bed with no backup set means either a late check-in or a cleaner idling on the clock. The linen-inventory logic has [its own full breakdown](/blog/linen-inventory-short-term-rental), and it starts at furnishing time, not the first time you run out.

One more reason the per-night number matters: taxes. In most jurisdictions furnishings are a capital asset you depreciate against rental income over several years, not an expense you write off in full the year you buy them (small-item and de-minimis rules vary, so this is a keep-every-receipt-and-ask-your-accountant flag, not tax advice). Either way, the money leaves your account once and comes back slowly — which is exactly why carrying it as a per-night cost, rather than a forgotten one-time hit, keeps your pricing honest.

## Where hosts overspend — and where they cheap out and pay for it

The pattern is remarkably consistent, and it's the mirror image of what the tiers tell you to do.

**Overspend:** the sofa nobody sleeps on but that "sets the tone," original art, a designer light fixture, a $600 dining table for a rental where guests eat takeout on it. None of it moves a booking or a review. A listing photo flattens a $2,000 sofa and a $600 one into the same rectangle of fabric.

**Cheap out:** the mattress (my $400 lesson), the wifi router, blackout curtains, enough pillows, a shower head with real pressure, and — the sleeper — enough matching cutlery and glasses that four guests can eat a meal without washing up mid-way. These are the exact things guests write about. A bad mattress is a recurring 4-star tax; a $70 router that drops under load turns a remote-worker guest into a one-star essay about "unusable internet."

The clean rule: **spend like a guest, not like an owner.** An owner optimizes for how the room looks to them on move-in day. A guest optimizes for eight hours of sleep, a hot shower, working wifi, and a kitchen that functions. Put your Tier-1 money there and let the decor be cheap and cheerful — it genuinely does not matter as much as the invoice suggests, and the review data proves it every single week.

When the place is furnished and live, the last thing standing between you and a clean launch is the calendar: a furnishing-week test booking on one platform has to block the others before a second guest grabs the same nights. ## FAQ

**How much does it cost to furnish an Airbnb?**
Budget roughly $7,000 to $7,500 to furnish a one-bedroom short-term rental from completely empty, buying new at mid-range prices. A studio runs a little less at around $6,000 because you still need every room's worth of items minus a wall, and each additional bedroom adds about $3,000 to $3,500. The figure swings with your city's prices and how much you buy used, but the shape holds: most of the money is in the shared rooms, not the bedrooms.

**What should I spend the most on when furnishing a rental?**
The mattress, the wifi, blackout curtains, water pressure, and a functional kitchen — the handful of things a guest names directly in a review. These are the items where the cheap version costs you more over a year in lower ratings and rebuys than the price gap to a good one. Spend the least on decorative pieces like art and cushions, which photograph the same and don't affect a single booking.

**How long does it take to recoup furnishing costs?**
In top-line terms, a one-bed's $7,500 furnishing is about 79 booked nights of gross revenue at a $95 average rate — under four months of a 55%-occupied calendar. Measured against actual profit after cleaning, commission, and utilities, the honest payback is closer to six to nine months in a healthy market. Either way furnishing is one of the fastest-returning investments in the business.

**Is furnishing a rental a one-time cost or ongoing?**
Both. You pay the bulk of it once, but furniture wears out on a schedule under guest use, so it becomes an ongoing cost too. Amortized over each item's replacement life — mattresses at six years, linens at about eighteen months, electronics at four — a $6,900 build works out to roughly $1,455 a year, or about $7.30 per booked night. That per-night figure belongs in your break-even calculation.

**How many sets of sheets and towels do I need per bed?**
Three complete sets per bed. One is on the bed, one is in the wash or clean in the closet, and one is the buffer that keeps a same-day turnover from stalling on a laundry cycle. Two sets works until you hit a back-to-back booking with a tight window; three sets means the cleaner is never waiting on the dryer and a check-in is never late because the sheets weren't ready.

**Should I buy new or used furniture for a short-term rental?**
Buy the review-critical and high-touch items new — the mattress, the sofa, cookware, the router — because guests are hard on them and reliability shows up in ratings. Buy the durable, low-scrutiny items used or flat-pack: dressers, nightstands, dining tables, side tables. Nobody has ever left a review scoring your chest of drawers, and a solid used dresser at a third of the price furnishes the room identically.

**Can I deduct furnishing costs from my rental taxes?**
In most jurisdictions furnishings are a capital asset you depreciate against rental income over several years rather than expense in full the year you buy them, though small-item and de-minimis safe-harbor rules differ by country and change often. Keep every receipt from day one and ask a local accountant how your jurisdiction treats it. The practical takeaway for pricing is the same regardless: the money comes back slowly, so carry it as a per-night cost.

**What's the difference between furnishing costs and consumables?**
Furnishing is the durable capital that stays with the property — beds, sofa, cookware, the TV. Consumables are the per-stay items a guest uses up and you restock: toilet paper, coffee, soap, dish tabs, trash bags. They're separate budget lines with separate logic — furnishing is a one-time capex you amortize, consumables are a recurring per-turnover cost. Mixing them makes both numbers wrong, which is why they get costed apart.

## One opinionated take

Almost every furnishing mistake is the same mistake: buying for the way the room looks to you on setup day instead of the way it works for a stranger at 2 a.m. The host who agonizes over a sofa's color and then buys the $200 mattress has it exactly backwards, because the guest will never mention the sofa and will absolutely mention the bed. Furnish for the review you're afraid of, not the photo you're proud of — put the money on the mattress, the wifi, and the blackout, let the decor be cheap, and the ratings will pay the difference back with interest.
