The first time my cleaner didn't show, I found out at 14:10 from the guest. Not from the cleaner — her car had died on the ring road and her phone had died with it. The guest was standing in the stairwell with two suitcases and a toddler, looking at an unmade bed through the door I had just unlocked remotely. That stay cost me one refunded night, a 3-star review, and roughly three hours of scrubbing in my work clothes.

The cleaner wasn't the problem. She had missed one job in fourteen months. The problem was that I had no way of knowing she was late until a guest told me, and no one to call when I did. A no-show is a rare event with an expensive tail, and the fix is not a better cleaner. It is a deadline, a bench, and a one-page handover.

## TL;DR

- A good cleaner still misses 1–3% of jobs. At 150 turnovers a year, that's 2–4.
- Only same-day turnovers are dangerous. A gap night turns a no-show into a reshuffle.
- The real damage is detection lag: noon vs 14:10 is the whole difference.
- Set a confirm-by-20:00 rule and a "started" ping by 11:30, then escalate at 12:00.
- Keep two backups who have each cleaned the unit at least twice this year.
- A bench costs about $90 a year in premiums. One unprepared miss: $300–$600.

## How often a reliable cleaner actually misses

Nobody publishes a no-show rate for independent cleaners, so here is the number from my own records and from the hosts I compare notes with: a cleaner you would happily recommend misses somewhere between 1 and 3 jobs in 100. Illness, a sick child, a car, a double-booked agency, a forgotten Sunday. Agencies are not immune — their failure mode is a substitute who has never seen the unit and arrives 90 minutes late with the wrong key.

Run that against your volume. One apartment at 70% occupancy with a 3.5-night average stay generates about 73 turnovers a year. Two apartments, about 150. At a 2% miss rate, that is 3 no-shows a year across your portfolio. Not a crisis, but not a once-a-decade event either. Plan for three.

The rate isn't evenly spread. Misses cluster on:

- **Sundays and public holidays**, when the cleaner's own family plans compete with yours.
- **Peak-season Saturdays**, when every host in town has same-day turnovers and agencies overbook staff.
- **The first job of the morning**, because a problem at 08:00 has no earlier job to surface it.
- **The first month with a new cleaner**, before the routine is a habit.

## Only same-day turnovers are expensive

A no-show matters only when the next guest arrives the same day. If the calendar has a free night between stays, a missed clean is an annoying text and a rescheduled job. Nobody refunds anything.

That makes your exposure a function of how many turnovers are back-to-back. At 50% occupancy, maybe a third of your turnovers are same-day. At 80% occupancy, most of them are. A host with two units running hot in August might have 40 same-day turnovers in a single month — that's where the 2% bites.

This is one more argument for the [buffer-day settings](/blog/cleaning-buffer-days) on units where turnover risk is highest: a gap night is insurance against more than slow cleaners. It also tells you where to spend effort. You don't need a backup plan for every turnover. You need one for the same-day turnovers on Saturdays and Sundays in high season.

## What a miss actually costs

Here is the tail, using a $150-a-night apartment as the example.

| Outcome | When it happens | Typical cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clean starts late, guest warned before noon | Detected by 12:00, backup found | $0–$30 goodwill credit |
| Check-in pushed 1–2 hours, guest warned at 14:00 | Detected late, backup found | $40–$75 credit, review risk |
| Guest arrives to a dirty unit | Not detected before arrival | One night refunded ($150), likely 3-star |
| Guest walks out and rebooks elsewhere | Dirty unit plus a demanding guest | Full stay refunded plus a relocation gap |
| Host cleans it personally | Any time, no backup | 3 hours of your day, plus the late check-in |

The 3-star is the expensive part, and it doesn't show on the payout. If your listing sits at 4.90, a single 3-star review takes 19 five-star stays to dilute back to 4.90 — the [rating recovery math](/blog/airbnb-rating-recovery-math) walks through the formula. At 5 reviews a month, that is about four months of perfect stays carrying the scar of one bad Saturday.

Put the table together and an unprepared miss averages $300–$600 once you price the review, depending on how far into the afternoon you find out. Three of those a year is $900–$1,800. That is the budget you are protecting.

## The real problem is detection lag

Look at the first two rows of the table again. The same no-show costs $0 if you know at 11:30 and $150 plus a review if you know at 14:10. The cleaner's reliability decides how often you have a problem; your detection decides how much each one costs.

Most hosts find out about a no-show from the guest because the only signal they have is the absence of a message. "No news" looks identical whether the cleaner is halfway through the bathroom or sitting in a tow truck. You need positive signals with deadlines.

The ladder I run now, for an 11:00 checkout and 15:00 check-in:

1. **Day before, 18:00** — the cleaner gets the next day's list with unit, checkout time, and the next arrival time.
2. **Day before, 20:00** — confirmation deadline. A thumbs-up, a "yes", anything. No reply by 20:00 means I call at 20:15, not at 11:00 tomorrow.
3. **Day of, 11:30** — "started" signal: the first photo of the unit as found, or a lock entry with the cleaner's own code. A smart lock that logs per-user codes gives you this signal for free.
4. **Day of, 12:00** — no start signal, no answer to one call: activate backup #1. Don't wait to be polite. At 12:00 a 2-bedroom turnover can still finish by 15:00; at 13:00 it can't.
5. **Day of, 12:30** — backup #1 hasn't confirmed: backup #2, then message the guest.
6. **Day of, completion** — 8–12 "finished" photos, same angles every time. That is your evidence if a guest later claims the place was dirty.

The 12:00 escalation time is not a magic number. Work it backwards: check-in time, minus the realistic turnover duration (2–3 hours for a 2-bedroom, plus 30 minutes of travel for a backup), minus 30 minutes of slack. For a 16:00 check-in you get an extra hour. For a 14:00 check-in you have almost none, which is a good reason not to offer 14:00 on Saturdays.

## Building a two-deep bench

A backup cleaner who has never been inside the unit is not a backup. They will spend the first 40 minutes finding the linen cupboard, the bin day, and the bathroom spray, and they will miss the things your guests notice: the coffee pods, the spare toilet roll, the remote batteries.

What makes a backup real:

- **They've cleaned the unit at least twice in the last 12 months.** Give each backup a normal paid job every few months — a deep clean, a day your primary is on holiday, a Sunday the primary prefers to skip. This costs you nothing extra; the job had to be done anyway.
- **They have their own access.** A dedicated lock code per backup, not your primary cleaner's code passed along by text. It also gives you a clean audit trail.
- **They have the one-page handover.** Address, door code, where linen and supplies live, bin day, the 10 things guests complain about, and 10 reference photos of the finished state from fixed angles.
- **They agreed to the emergency rate in advance.** Haggling at 12:05 wastes the 25 minutes you don't have. Agree up front: same-day call-outs pay 1.5x.

Two backups, not one. Backup #1 will be unavailable on exactly the days you need them, because those are the busy days for every cleaner in town. The second name is what turns a bench from a nice idea into actual cover.

Where to find them: the other cleaners your cleaner knows (ask — most cleaners have a friend they swap shifts with), another host's cleaner for a reciprocal arrangement, and an on-demand cleaning marketplace as the last line. Turno and similar marketplaces work as a third option, but a stranger booked at noon is the slowest and least reliable choice, not the first.

Don't forget the human side with your primary. Tell them a backup exists and why. A cleaner who learns about the backup by seeing someone else's photos on their unit will assume they're being replaced.

## The bench math

Let's price it for two apartments with about 150 turnovers a year, a $60 standard clean, and a 2% miss rate.

| Line | Without a bench | With a two-deep bench |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expected misses per year | 3 | 3 |
| Familiarisation jobs for backups | — | 4 per year, $0 extra (replace primary's jobs) |
| Emergency premium (1.5x on $60) | — | 3 × $30 = $90 |
| Handover photos and lock codes | — | One afternoon, once |
| Refunds and credits | 3 × $150 = $450 | 3 × $15 average = $45 |
| Review damage (priced, rough) | $450–$1,350 | Close to $0 |
| **Total per year** | **$900–$1,800** | **about $135** |

The review line is the soft one; even if you price it at zero, the bench still wins on refunds alone. And the comparison ignores your own time. Cleaning a 2-bedroom yourself in an emergency is three hours you weren't planning to lose, usually on a Saturday.

If you run a single unit with mostly gap nights, the case is weaker — you might see one dangerous miss every two years. One backup with a handover sheet is enough. The two-deep bench pays for itself somewhere around the second unit, or the first summer at 75%+ occupancy.

## Telling the guest before they find out

If the escalation ladder fails and the unit will not be ready on time, the message to the guest decides whether this becomes a credit or a refund. Send it as soon as you know, not at 14:55.

What works, in this order:

1. **The new time, specific.** "Your apartment will be ready at 16:30", not "slightly delayed".
2. **What they can do meanwhile.** Luggage drop in the hallway or at a nearby locker, a café recommendation, the parking spot.
3. **A small credit, offered, not negotiated.** $25–$50 for a 1–2 hour delay on a $150 night. Offer it in the first message; a credit offered after a complaint reads as damage control.
4. **No excuses about the cleaner.** The guest didn't hire the cleaner. "Our cleaning team is running late" is enough.

A guest warned at noon, with a time and a credit, almost never mentions the delay in the review. A guest who discovers it on the doorstep almost always does. If the delay was handled through the platform's messaging, keep the thread — it documents that you notified them early if they later open a claim.

## FAQ

**What should I do if my Airbnb cleaner doesn't show up?**

Call the cleaner once, then call your first backup immediately — don't wait more than 15 minutes for a reply. If no backup is free by 12:30 for a 15:00 check-in, message the guest with a specific new ready time and a small credit, then clean it yourself or book an on-demand cleaner as the last resort.

**How do I know if my cleaner has started the turnover?**

Ask for a positive signal, not silence. The simplest is a first photo of the unit as found, sent before a fixed time such as 11:30. A smart lock with a dedicated code per cleaner also logs the entry time automatically, which gives you the signal without asking for anything.

**How many backup cleaners does an Airbnb host need?**

Two per area for anyone with same-day turnovers in high season. One backup is unavailable on exactly the busy days you need them. A single unit with mostly gap nights can get by with one backup and a handover sheet.

**How much should I pay a backup cleaner for a same-day emergency?**

1.5 times the standard rate is the common arrangement, agreed in advance. On a $60 clean that is $90. Paying it is far cheaper than a refunded night, and agreeing it upfront means you don't negotiate at noon.

**Should I compensate the guest if the cleaning is late?**

Yes, if check-in moves by an hour or more. $25–$50 for a 1–2 hour delay on a $150 night is typical. Offer it in the first message together with a specific new time; offered before the guest complains, it usually keeps the delay out of the review.

**Can I charge my cleaner for a no-show?**

Penalties for independent cleaners rarely work and push good cleaners away. The practical consequence is losing jobs to the backup if no-shows repeat. One miss in a year is normal; two unannounced misses in a month is a reason to promote your backup.

**What should a cleaning handover sheet include?**

Address and access code, where linen, supplies, and spare consumables live, bin collection day, a checklist of the 10 things guests most often complain about, and 8–12 reference photos of the finished state taken from fixed angles. One page is enough.

**Is a gap night between stays worth it just to avoid cleaner problems?**

On its own, rarely — it costs a sellable night. But on high-risk days, like Sundays and holiday weekends in peak season, a minimum gap or a later check-in time is cheap insurance if your bench is thin.

## One opinionated take

The right question about a cleaner is not "how reliable are they" but "how fast would I know if they weren't". A 99%-reliable cleaner with no deadline and no backup costs you more than a 97%-reliable one with a 20:00 confirmation, a 12:00 escalation, and two people who know where the coffee pods live.
